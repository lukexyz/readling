# What we’re learning from the Word Woods

Readling is a game for a four-year-old who has started phonics at school. The aim is for her to look at an unfamiliar word, work it out and enjoy finding out what it means. One word can be a complete session, on either her colour tablet or the black-and-white Daylight.

## What we want her to be able to do

- See the lowercase `c` in *cat*, say its /k/ sound and try forming it with a stylus or finger. She can move on whenever she’s ready.
- Work from left to right, say the sounds for `c`, `a` and `t`, then join them and say *cat* before revealing the picture.
- Notice what changes when `c` becomes `h` in *hat*. Later, try the same skill with a word she has never collected.
- Come back to a word another day, give it a go from the print alone, then pick its picture from look-alikes. She can ask for a sound when she needs one.

## Why the game works this way

**Start with sounds she knows.** The first collection has 12 words, each with three letters and three sounds. A grown-up can select the sounds covered in class so every new word is within reach. The letter shelf models individual sounds. “Blend with me” plays one continuous, slowed word while the letters light up in order. In a study with beginning readers, children who practised joining sounds continuously were more accurate when decoding unfamiliar letter strings. [Development Matters, literacy guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/development-matters--2/development-matters#literacy) · [Gonzalez-Frey’s blending study](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/gc_etds/3860/)

**Give her a chance to try, then make help easy.** We considered a three-tap limit for the alphabet. The shelf stays available so she can hear a tricky sound again and carry on. A new word comes back after two minutes. Memory needs two finds and deals up to four cards at a time, with the printed word facing up. “Read with me” says the word and brings that card back in about two minutes. After a wrong picture she hears the slow blend and tries again, so every card ends on the right answer.

**Let the picture show what she read.** The first version asked her to press “I read it”. Young children tend to rate their own work generously, so the card now checks. She reads the print, turns the card and taps its picture from up to three in her album. The other two are look-alikes, such as *cup* and *hat* for *cat*, so the first letter alone cannot settle it. The pictures stay hidden until the card turns, because in small studies a picture shown beside a word got in the way of learning the word. Only her first tap counts. [Van Loon and colleagues on children’s self-evaluations](https://boris.unibe.ch/108586) · [Singh and Solman’s picture-blocking study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1286268)

**Follow the sounds as well as the words.** Each letter-sound keeps its own dates. When her first tap is right with no hints, every sound in that word that was due rests for 1, 3, 7, 14 and eventually 30 days. A wrong picture marks the sounds that differ: tapping *cup* for *cat* marks `a` and `t` and leaves `c` alone. Asking the shelf for a sound in the word marks that sound. Marked sounds return in about two minutes, in the missed word and in a second word that uses them when she has one. When cards are due, the main button offers memory before a new word. One finished round is enough until her next find, and a grown-up can switch this off. In one classroom study, short bursts of phonics spread through the day beat a single longer session. Those intervals are a choice to revisit after we see her play. [Seabrook, Brown and Solity on spaced practice](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2005-seabrook.pdf)

**Make the discovery hers.** Her own handwriting goes into the album alongside the illustration. A new word earns five stars, and every third discovery brings a level. She can replay her show with Encore, and a grown-up can mute its music or turn the show off. The story line gives a grown-up an opening for conversation, such as “Where do you think that cat is going?” [What Works Clearinghouse guidance on letters, sounds and shared reading](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/PracticeGuide/30)

**Build the reveal from her own sounds.** When she taps “Reveal my word” the page dims and the stage is empty. Her own handwritten letters land on it one at a time, each with its sound. Then a star strikes the stage, her picture appears and the whole word is spoken. She sees and hears sounds become a word and the word become a thing. The idea comes from a close study of how the game Peggle celebrates a finished level: it announces the win a moment before it happens, and it makes the celebration out of what the player just did.

**Let her build the celebration.** The show starts small: the hush, her letters, the star and one burst. Her first find or memory round earns a choice of two things to add, and every second one after that earns another. The first choice is the rainbow or the music. There are 23 things in all, from more stars and growing flowers to dolphins, marching elephants and a whale. Each choice is a moving picture with its name under it, and one tap adds it and plays it by itself. The one she leaves is offered again next time, and nothing she adds is ever taken away. Her finds join in too: the things in her album can parade along the bottom, fireflies can spell her word, and the last firework can take the shape of what she found. A memory round now ends with her show as well, because coming back to practise is where a reason to return helps most.

**Keep it short by taking turns.** Peggle fits twelve events into fifteen seconds because each begins as the last one settles. The show follows that. Things in the sky and on the ground play every time. The visitors and the big finishes take turns, two of each per show, so the line-up changes from one show to the next. Her stars are counted out one by one and fly up to her total, and finishing a collection of three pins a rosette to the stage.

## What building and testing taught us

- The first narrated words were about 20 dB quieter than the fanfare. Matching their volume mattered as much as choosing the voice. We kept human recordings for the isolated sounds and slow blends. The 12 spoken words, 12 story lines and four prompts are generated once and shipped as MP3s. Public replays use zero ElevenLabs credits.
- Her album lives in the browser she plays in. A grown-up can export it from one tablet and import it on the other. A mistyped address on the tablet produced a secure-connection error, so a tappable link or QR code is an easier handoff. [Open the public game](https://readling-word-woods.skuxnet.chatgpt.site/)
- The picture check reuses the “Say the word, then turn the card” prompt, so it needed no new recordings. “Tap the picture for your word” appears on screen and is not spoken yet.
- With nothing added the show runs about five and a half seconds, and about seven with four things. With all 23 it runs 14 to 16 seconds, depending on which visitors and finishes take their turn. Playing every one of them, which only Grown-ups can do, takes 20.
- The choices for her show are not spoken yet either. Each is a moving picture with its name printed under it. Speaking them needs new recordings.
- Peggle lets a drum roll enter alone before its music. We have no roll, so the hush is silent apart from her letter sounds. The fanfare is still the one recording and plays after her word once she has added music.
- Grown-ups has a try-out mode for the show. Any mix of its parts can be ticked and played without changing her show, and it reports how long that show runs, when her picture appears and when each part plays. It is how we find out which parts are worth keeping.
- The show keeps real time on a slow tablet by taking several small steps for each frame it manages to draw. An early version slowed down instead, and its sounds drifted away from its pictures.

## What to watch for in play

Stars tell us she participated. Sound progress in Grown-ups shows, for each sound, how often she read it unaided and where she slipped. A right picture can be a lucky guess about one time in three, so look for a pattern over several days. Memory only uses words from her album, so to see whether the skill carries over, a grown-up can write *sat* on paper once she knows those three sounds and listen to how she works it out. Notice how she takes to memory coming first. If it gets in the way of wanting another word, a grown-up can switch it off. On the Daylight, watch whether she can follow the active letter and see her pen marks in grey. Watch whether the hush before the picture holds her or makes her impatient, and whether she starts hurrying through a word to reach her show. Notice what she chooses to add and whether she looks for it next time. The rainbow and the paint are colour ideas and are the weakest on the Daylight. After the show, notice what she chooses next. Asking for another word, showing someone her writing or happily closing the album are all good ways for a session to end.
